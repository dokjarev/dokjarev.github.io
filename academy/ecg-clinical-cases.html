ECG reasoning in clinical practice

Four situations in which the same electrical recording serves very different clinical questions: chest pain, sudden collapse, reperfusion and difficult separation from bypass.

Clinical reasoning series 28 October 2026

These cases deliberately begin before the ECG appears. Each first asks you to name the important clinical problem and predict which electrical features would matter. Only then does it introduce findings, a competing explanation, and the limitations of the test.

How to use the cases

  1. Read the clinical trigger and pause. Decide what is most urgent, and what immediate action might already be necessary.
  2. State one working diagnosis and at least one serious alternative.
  3. Predict the specific ECG evidence you would seek, before reading the discussion.
  4. Open the reasoning and pitfalls section. Identify what additional evidence is needed and whether your initial hypothesis is still plausible.
  5. After the urgent question is answered, use the systematic second pass from Part I.

These are educational thought exercises, not real patient records or substitutes for clinical decision pathways. The source-guideline links are placed directly beside the relevant clinical reasoning.

Case 1 · Emergency department

Chest pain with ongoing symptoms

Clinical trigger: A patient develops severe central chest discomfort with diaphoresis. Symptoms persist at first medical assessment.

Ischemia?Acute coronary occlusion?Other life threats?
What must be considered first?
Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), including an acute coronary occlusion that may require urgent reperfusion assessment. Do not ignore alternative causes of chest pain and hemodynamic compromise.
What ECG findings would you predict?
New ST-segment elevation or equivalent ischemic patterns, ST depression, T-wave changes, reciprocal changes or dynamic changes across contiguous leads. Which pattern would require activation of the appropriate pathway?
New information
The first ECG is nondiagnostic, but the pain continues.
What can you conclude?
A nondiagnostic initial recording cannot safely exclude ACS. The 2025 ACC/AHA multisociety ACS guideline recommends timely ECG acquisition and serial 12-lead ECGs when symptoms persist, suspicion remains high, or the condition deteriorates. Biomarkers and the full clinical assessment are also needed.
Reasoning and pitfalls

Potential anchoring: assuming that the absence of classic ST elevation rules out an occlusive culprit lesion or ACS. In a review of ECG reasoning, Nunes de Alencar et al. (2024) emphasize that ECG signs are imperfect surrogate markers of disease. The 2023 ESC ACS guideline likewise structures initial clinical pathways around the ECG without treating a nondiagnostic trace as exclusion of ACS.

Learning question: Which negative ECG finding would genuinely lower your suspicion, and which merely fails to confirm the first hypothesis?

Case 2 · Resuscitation

Sudden collapse

Clinical trigger: An adult becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally. An emergency response is activated.

Cardiac arrest?Shockable rhythm?Perfusing rhythm?
What must be determined first?
Recognition of cardiac arrest and initiation of the appropriate resuscitation response. Rapid breathing and pulse assessment must not become an extended diagnostic examination.
Which electrical distinction changes management?
After the monitor/defibrillator is attached, ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia is a shockable category; PEA and asystole are non-shockable categories. The distinction determines the resuscitation branch.
New information
The monitor shows organized complexes, yet no definite pulse is present.
What can you conclude?
Electrical organization is not proof of mechanical circulation. Continue assessment and resuscitation according to the 2025 AHA Adult Advanced Life Support guidance; do not defer CPR to obtain a conventional 12-lead ECG.
Reasoning and pitfalls

Potential anchoring: seeing a familiar sinus-like monitor rhythm and assuming adequate circulation. The useful question here is “Is this a perfusing rhythm?”, rather than beginning with axis, QTc or chamber criteria.

Learning question: In what situation does an ECG pattern represent a rhythm category but fail to establish the physiological state of the patient? The 2025 AHA arrest algorithm provides the reference pathways.

Case 3 · Cardiac operating room

Immediately after aortic cross-clamp removal

Clinical trigger: During cardiac surgery, the aortic cross-clamp is removed. Organized electrical and mechanical recovery is not promptly apparent while extracorporeal support continues.

VF or severe arrhythmia?Conduction or metabolic problem?Myocardial recovery?
What is the immediate problem representation?
Delayed recovery after reperfusion. Identify the rhythm and whether there is effective myocardial activity, considering the level of ongoing bypass support.
What ECG evidence would help?
Distinguish organized QRS complexes from ventricular fibrillation or other significant rhythm disturbances; examine QRS widening or newly abnormal conduction.
What alternatives require consideration?
Residual cardioplegic effects, electrolyte or acid–base abnormalities, ischemia, possible coronary air and other operative or reperfusion-related factors. Do not infer a specific mechanism from the ECG alone.
What additional information matters?
Myocardial activity, perfusion and pressure, the operative timeline, cardioplegia history, blood gases/electrolytes, and imaging when clinically appropriate. The 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP CPB guideline discusses perfusion, weaning and perioperative physiological factors; this vignette is an original teaching synthesis, not a guideline-derived algorithm for declamping.
Reasoning and pitfalls

Potential anchoring: labeling a wide-complex rhythm as “hyperkalemia” before checking whether it reflects an arrhythmia, reperfusion-related ischemia, temperature, conduction injury, or other causes.

Learning question: Which observation distinguishes electrical disorganization from mechanically ineffective organized activity? Which immediate tests would change the team's interpretation?

Case 4 · Cardiac operating room

Failure to separate from cardiopulmonary bypass

Clinical trigger: After an attempted reduction in bypass support, the circulation remains inadequate and separation cannot be completed without escalating intervention.

Pump failure?Ischemia?Vasoplegia / loading?Mechanical problem?
What is the first clinical question?
Which physiologic mechanism prevents stable native circulation: ventricular dysfunction, a rhythm disturbance, inadequate loading, vasodilation, ischemia or a structural/surgical problem?
What can the ECG contribute?
Identify a rhythm or conduction problem and look for evolving ST abnormalities, ideally compared with the pre-bypass tracing.
New information
The monitor shows a plausible regular rhythm, yet arterial pressure remains inadequate during weaning.
What can you conclude?
An electrically organized rhythm does not establish sufficient cardiac output. The EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline's separation-from-CPB section recommends multidisciplinary, structured assessment and emphasizes the role of transesophageal echocardiography, loading conditions and dynamic or structural abnormalities.
Reasoning and pitfalls

Potential anchoring: treating a postoperative ECG or normal-looking monitor rhythm as proof that the myocardium is functioning adequately. Hemodynamic measurements and TOE may be far more decisive for identifying the mechanism in this setting.

Learning question: What would regional wall-motion abnormalities suggest? What alternative explanation would you consider if ventricular function were preserved but systemic vascular resistance were low?

The goal is to improve the sequence notice the clinical problem → predict the expected signal → interrogate the ECG → consider alternatives → integrate physiology. Gupta et al. (2024) motivate combining pattern recognition and inductive–deductive reasoning. For protection against bias, return to the structured second pass in Part I; evidence summarized by Oh et al. (2022) supports systematic ECG training.
