Clinical-first ECG interpretation

When a patient is deteriorating, you do not begin with the PR interval. You begin with a problem, a dangerous possibility, and the ECG evidence that would change what you do next.

Clinical reasoning guide · Version 0.2 28 October 2026 Case-based learning

A systematic ECG interpretation is valuable: it teaches you not to overlook important findings. But a patient does not present as a blank ECG. The clinical situation usually tells you which diagnosis you must confirm or urgently consider first. The skill to develop is moving deliberately between that first hypothesis, the available ECG evidence and alternative explanations.

Start with a question, not a checklist: “Given what is happening to this patient, what is the most dangerous plausible diagnosis that I need to identify now?”

Then ask: “What should the ECG show if I am right? What ECG finding would make another explanation more likely? What can the ECG not exclude?”

1. From the bedside to the ECG

01RecognizeWhat happened? What is the patient's perfusion and stability?
02NameState the leading time-critical diagnosis and one dangerous alternative.
03PredictWhich specific ECG features should be present if that diagnosis is correct?
04TestInspect the relevant leads, rhythm, intervals or pattern; compare with prior data.
05Decide & revisitAct if needed, check other evidence and perform a systematic second pass.

These steps can take seconds in an emergency and much longer in a stable assessment. An unstable patient needs appropriate immediate treatment while the diagnostic question is being refined. The first hypothesis is a starting point, not a conclusion.

2. Four clinical situations, four different questions

Emergency department

A patient with ongoing chest pain

First diagnosis to consider
Acute coronary syndrome, particularly an acute coronary occlusion requiring urgent reperfusion assessment.
What to look for immediately
Obtain a 12-lead ECG promptly. Assess ST elevation or equivalent ischemic patterns, changes in anatomically related leads, reciprocal changes and dynamic evolution.
What would change the plan?
Findings compatible with a time-critical occlusive event trigger the appropriate ACS pathway; a nondiagnostic tracing with ongoing suspicion calls for repeat ECGs and the rest of the diagnostic workup.

Critical limitation: a normal or nondiagnostic first ECG does not exclude ACS. [4]

Sudden collapse / resuscitation

A patient collapses and is unresponsive

First decision
Is this cardiac arrest? Assess responsiveness and breathing, and determine pulse status promptly according to resuscitation training; initiate CPR and the emergency response if indicated.
What to look for immediately
Once a monitor/defibrillator is attached, distinguish a shockable rhythm (VF or pulseless VT) from a non-shockable rhythm (PEA or asystole).
What would change the plan?
The rhythm category determines the arrest-management branch. Simultaneously search for reversible causes and reassess evidence of circulation.

Critical limitation: organized electrical activity does not guarantee a pulse. Do not delay resuscitation to obtain a conventional 12-lead ECG. [5]

Cardiac operating room

The aortic cross-clamp has just been removed

First diagnosis to consider
Failure of organized electrical and mechanical recovery after reperfusion: identify VF, severe bradyarrhythmia or conduction failure, and whether circulation is adequate on current support.
What to look for immediately
Inspect the rhythm for organized complexes versus fibrillation, and the QRS for new widening or conduction changes; correlate with arterial pressure, myocardial activity, temperature and perfusion.
What else must be checked?
Consider effects of recent cardioplegia, electrolyte disturbances (including potassium), coronary air, ischemia and the timing of myocardial recovery. The operative team uses direct observation, blood gases and echocardiography as appropriate.

Critical limitation: the ECG does not, by itself, reveal why the myocardium has failed to recover. [6]

Cardiac operating room

Difficulty separating from cardiopulmonary bypass

First diagnosis to consider
Ask whether the main problem is cardiac pump failure, ischemia, an unstable rhythm or another cause of inadequate circulation.
What to look for immediately
Identify new rhythm or conduction abnormalities and evolving ST changes; compare with the pre-bypass ECG, if available.
What else must be checked?
Use hemodynamics and transesophageal echocardiography to evaluate ventricular performance, regional wall motion and alternative causes such as loading conditions, vasoplegia or surgical complications. [6]

Critical limitation: a rhythm or ST abnormality is only one component of the multidisciplinary assessment.

Notice what changes across the cases: the first question. In chest pain it may be acute ischemia; in cardiac arrest it is the rhythm category and perfusion; after cross-clamp release it is electrical and mechanical recovery. The underlying ECG knowledge is the same, but different features deserve immediate attention.

3. Know the ECG tools that test your hypothesis

Clinical-first does not mean skipping fundamentals. You need a rapid, accurate understanding of the tracing to make the first hypothesis testable. The following is a toolbox, not a compulsory order for every clinical situation.

Clinical questionECG feature to examineImportant limitation
Is the rhythm causing instability?Rate, regularity, P waves, QRS morphology and pulse relationship.Monitor rhythm alone does not establish perfusion.
Could this be a conduction disturbance?PR relationship, dropped ventricular complexes, QRS duration and escape rhythm.Context, medication and reversible causes matter.
Could this be myocardial ischemia?ST-T changes across related leads, reciprocal patterns and serial evolution.One ECG can be nondiagnostic; findings are not specific in isolation.
Could technical factors explain the appearance?Electrode placement, artifact, calibration, baseline and comparison tracings.Correcting technique does not exclude true simultaneous pathology.

4. The 12-lead recording: a technical foundation

The standard resting 12-lead ECG uses 10 surface electrodes: four limb electrodes and six precordial (chest) electrodes. Those electrical measurements are combined into 12 leads, or views of cardiac electrical activity. An electrode is a physical sensor; a lead is a particular view derived from the recorded signals. When a waveform looks implausible, consider electrode position, a cable connection problem, movement, muscular activity or electrical noise. A misplaced electrode can produce misleading axis, polarity or morphology. Always check identification, recording speed, gain and recording quality before drawing conclusions. [1]

5. A systematic second pass: protect against anchoring

Once the urgent clinical question has been addressed, take a deliberate second look for findings outside your first hypothesis. This matters particularly when the patient is stable enough for a complete recording or when the first diagnosis does not explain the physiology.

RhythmReview atrial activity, regularity, ventricular rate, and atrioventricular relationships. Is a second arrhythmia present?
ConductionCheck PR, QRS and QT/QTc in context. As rough adult reference points, the PR interval is usually around 120–200 ms and QRS duration of 120 ms or more is wide. Corrected QT values depend on measurement and correction method. [2]
Orientation and morphologyReview frontal axis, P-wave morphology, Q waves, progression of precordial R waves and abnormalities of ST segments and T waves in related leads.
VerificationCheck technical quality and previous tracings. Decide which findings remain unexplained and what additional information is needed.

A complete read is valuable as a cognitive safety net, rather than as a requirement to delay a time-critical decision.

6. A technical trap: display gain

A waveform may appear to shrink when voltage gain is reduced, even though the underlying measured signal is unchanged. This is why an unexpectedly low-amplitude tracing should prompt a calibration check alongside clinical assessment.

7. Practise the reasoning, not only pattern recognition

To learn this skill, use a small exercise before looking at an ECG. It trains you to move from a clinical trigger to a testable question rather than retrospectively naming a pattern you already see.

  1. Read the clinical situation first. Identify the most urgent change in physiology, and whether immediate treatment is required.
  2. Commit to one working diagnosis. Say it aloud. Also name an alternative that would require a different response.
  3. Predict the ECG finding. Choose the lead group, interval or rhythm pattern that would support your hypothesis.
  4. Look at the recording. Specify whether your prediction was supported, contradicted or left unresolved.
  5. Check what the ECG cannot tell you. Identify the next source of evidence—exam, hemodynamics, echocardiography, laboratory results, serial ECGs—and revisit the hypothesis.
  6. Finish with a second pass. Look for important abnormalities that did not belong to your first explanation.

Crucially, an absent expected ECG sign is only useful as exclusion evidence when the test is sensitive enough in that setting. For example, absence of ST elevation alone does not exclude acute coronary syndrome. [3] [4]

8. Try it: the first 15 seconds of reasoning

Scenario: Immediately after aortic cross-clamp removal, the heart shows sluggish electrical recovery and the monitor shows broad ventricular complexes. You are still supported by the heart–lung machine.

Before measuring every interval, answer three questions: (1) What time-critical problem must you clarify? (2) Which two mechanisms could explain it? (3) What evidence other than the ECG could distinguish them?

Compare with one possible reasoning pathway

Working problem: Is there an unstable ventricular rhythm or a conduction/metabolic disturbance preventing effective recovery?

Competing explanations: the effects of potassium-containing cardioplegia or another electrolyte disturbance, myocardial ischemia or coronary air, among others.

Next evidence: identify the rhythm and QRS pattern while checking direct myocardial activity, perfusion and pressures; review blood gas/electrolyte information and transesophageal echo findings within the operative team. Treat time-critical instability according to the cardiac-surgery team's established protocols. [6]

This is not a diagnosis from one tracing. It is an example of making the first question explicit.

Five questions to take to the bedside

  1. What changed in this patient?
  2. Which dangerous diagnosis is highest priority to identify?
  3. What ECG sign should I actively look for?
  4. What alternate diagnosis or missing finding changes my interpretation?
  5. What do I need to do or verify next?
