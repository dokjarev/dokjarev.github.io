ECG interpretation essentials

From electrical recording to clinical meaning: a practical framework for reading a 12-lead electrocardiogram without losing sight of the patient.

Public working draft · Version 0.1 28 October 2026 ~10-minute read

A 12-lead ECG can reveal important abnormalities of heart rate, rhythm, conduction and repolarization. Yet interpretation begins before recognizing a waveform. Was the tracing acquired correctly? What is the clinical question? Does a finding explain the patient's symptoms? This guide develops a deliberately simple approach for learners, not a definitive diagnostic algorithm.

1. Understand what the ECG actually records

The standard resting 12-lead ECG uses 10 surface electrodes: four limb electrodes and six precordial (chest) electrodes. Those electrical measurements are combined into 12 leads, or views of cardiac electrical activity. An electrode is a physical sensor; a lead is a particular view derived from the recorded signals. When a waveform looks implausible, consider electrode position, a cable connection problem, movement, muscular activity or electrical noise. A misplaced electrode can produce misleading axis, polarity or morphology. Always check identification, recording speed, gain and recording quality before drawing conclusions. [1]

These are common standard settings, not guarantees. If the speed or gain differs, the appearance of intervals and amplitudes changes.

2. Read in a consistent sequence

Instead of memorizing isolated patterns, ask a small number of repeatable questions. Each answer narrows the next question.

First: rate and rhythmCount the ventricular rate and examine the R–R intervals. Is the rhythm regular, irregularly irregular or patterned? Identify atrial activity: do P waves precede each QRS, and is the relationship consistent? A pulse rate and an electrical heart rate may not always match.
Next: orientation and conductionEstimate the frontal QRS axis, then inspect the PR interval, QRS duration and QT/QTc. As rough adult reference points, PR is usually about 120–200 ms and a QRS duration of 120 ms or more is considered wide. QT interpretation depends on heart rate, correction method, patient characteristics and the measurement itself. Avoid treating a computer-generated QTc as unquestionable. [2]
Then: waveforms and repolarizationLook across related leads for P-wave features, QRS morphology, abnormal Q waves, R-wave progression, ST-segment shifts and T-wave abnormalities. Describe what is present before naming a diagnosis; a single unusual wave is rarely the whole story.
Finally: the clinical contextCompare with a prior ECG when available. Consider symptoms, vital signs, medication effects, electrolytes, relevant history and examination findings. Ask whether a repeat ECG, additional leads, monitoring or further assessment is warranted. An ECG is evidence, not a stand-alone diagnosis.

3. A useful trap: recording settings can look like disease

A waveform may appear to shrink when voltage gain is reduced, even though the underlying measured signal is unchanged. This is why an unexpectedly low-amplitude tracing should prompt a calibration check alongside clinical assessment.

4. Recognize findings that change urgency

A concerning ECG must be evaluated with the patient's condition. For example, syncope with marked bradycardia, high-grade or complete AV block, a dangerous tachyarrhythmia, or dynamic changes suggesting acute myocardial ischemia may require urgent assessment. Do not assume every bradycardia warrants pacing: the type of conduction disturbance, potential reversible causes and clinical context matter. [2]

In suspected acute coronary syndrome, guidelines emphasize prompt 12-lead recording and interpretation, with repeat ECGs when the first tracing is nondiagnostic or symptoms evolve. A normal-looking initial ECG does not rule out an acute coronary syndrome. These decisions belong to clinical protocols and qualified teams. [3] [4]

5. One-minute self-check

Thought experiment: Two consecutive ECG printouts from the same stable patient show visibly different QRS heights. The displayed heart rate, QRS duration and waveform timing appear unchanged. On the first printout the calibration reads 10 mm/mV; on the second, 5 mm/mV. What is the first technical explanation to consider?

Reveal explanation

At 5 mm/mV, the same measured voltage occupies half the vertical distance on paper. Verify calibration before interpreting the apparent amplitude difference as a clinical change. This example does not exclude simultaneous real disease; it illustrates why recording conditions should be checked first.

At the bedside, remember this sequence

  1. Verify the recording and its calibration.
  2. Describe the rate, rhythm, conduction and waveforms.
  3. Compare related leads and previous ECGs.
  4. Interpret the tracing in the patient's clinical context.
  5. Act according to urgency and appropriate clinical guidance.

Scope and editorial status

This is a public working draft (v0.1) for undergraduate and early postgraduate education. It is intentionally introductory, is not yet independently peer reviewed, and does not replace formal ECG training, specialist interpretation or local emergency-care protocols. Thresholds and examples are primarily for adults. Future updates may add independently created case exercises, lead-placement diagrams and detailed AV block comparisons.
